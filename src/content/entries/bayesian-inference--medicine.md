title: "What does a positive medical test really mean?"
method: bayesian-inference
field: medicine
status: reviewed
difficulty: intro
summary: >-
  A positive test result does not always mean a patient is sick. How likely they
  really are to have the disease depends on two things: how accurate the test is,
  and how common the disease was to begin with. Bayes' rule combines these, turning
  the chance of disease before the test (the "prior") into the chance after the
  test (the "posterior"). Doctors use this reasoning every day when they decide
  whether to order more tests or start treatment. Researchers have written guides
  showing how to apply Bayes' rule to diagnosis, how to use "likelihood ratios" and
  a simple chart to update a patient's probability, and how heart surgery trials use
  the same idea to update beliefs about whether a treatment works as new patient
  data comes in.
starterQuestion: >-
  A disease affects 1 in 1,000 people. A test for it catches 99% of people who have
  it and wrongly flags 5% of people who don't. If someone tests positive, what is
  the chance they actually have the disease? Work it out with a table of 100,000
  imaginary people, then check it with Bayes' rule. How does the answer change if
  the disease affects 1 in 10 people, or if the person takes a second test?
