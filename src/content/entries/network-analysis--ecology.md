title: "Food webs, pollinators, and what holds an ecosystem together"
method: network-analysis
field: ecology
status: reviewed
difficulty: intro
summary: >-
  Every species in an ecosystem is linked to others: wolves eat deer, deer eat
  plants, and bees carry pollen between flowers. Ecologists draw these
  relationships as a network, with each species as a dot (a node) and each
  feeding or pollinating relationship as a line (a link). Simple counts then
  describe the whole ecosystem. One is connectance: the share of all possible
  links that actually exist. Comparing 16 real food webs showed that they are
  much more tightly connected than most other networks scientists study. Network
  tools can also measure change: a three-year field experiment found that adding
  beehives to a natural area pushed out wild pollinators, removed links from the
  network, and left some plants producing fewer seeds.
starterQuestion: >-
  Build a small food web of about 10 species from a habitat near you (a pond, a
  forest, or a school field) using field guides or online sources. Calculate its
  connectance: the number of feeding links divided by the number of possible
  links. Then remove one species at a time and count how many other species lose
  all of their food. Which species, when removed, causes the most damage, and is
  it the one with the most links?
