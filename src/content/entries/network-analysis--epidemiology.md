title: "Tracing how diseases spread through who meets whom"
method: network-analysis
field: epidemiology
status: reviewed
difficulty: intro
summary: >-
  Many diseases, like the flu, spread when people are close to each other. A
  network turns this into a picture you can do math on: each person is a dot (a
  node), and a line (a link) joins two people who spend time near each other.
  Counting a person's links tells you how many people they could infect, and
  some people turn out to be far more connected than others. Researchers have
  put small wireless sensors on everyone at an American high school for a day,
  built the network of close contacts, and simulated a flu outbreak on it. The
  simulation matched the real pattern of student absences, and it showed that
  using the network to decide who gets vaccinated works better than vaccinating
  people at random. The same tools are used to track disease among farm animals
  and wildlife, where trade and herd behavior decide who meets whom.
starterQuestion: >-
  Draw a made-up contact network for a class of 30 students, where each student
  has between 2 and 10 contacts. Start a flu outbreak with one sick student and
  simulate it: each day, every sick student has a 20% chance of infecting each of
  their contacts. Repeat the outbreak many times. Does vaccinating the 5 students
  with the most contacts protect the class better than vaccinating 5 students
  chosen at random? By how much?

  - "Drawing and reading simple graphs of dots and lines"
