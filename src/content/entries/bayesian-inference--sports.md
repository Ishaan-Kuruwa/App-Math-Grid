title: "Judging players and predicting matches when data is scarce"
method: bayesian-inference
field: sports
status: reviewed
difficulty: intermediate
summary: >-
  Sports data is often thin: a rookie has played only a few games, and an elite
  team has only a handful of athletes to study. Bayesian inference helps by
  starting from what is already known (a "prior", such as how an average player
  performs) and updating it with the new data to get a "posterior", a range of
  likely values instead of a single guess. Researchers have used this to test
  whether ice baths help soccer players recover their sprint speed, borrowing
  knowledge from earlier studies to make up for a small group of athletes. Others
  have surveyed how Bayesian methods are used across many sports, and built models
  that estimate each team's attacking and defending strength to predict the scores
  of Italian league football matches.
starterQuestion: >-
  A baseball player gets 8 hits in their first 20 at-bats, a .400 average. What is
  your best estimate of their true batting average? Use the league average as your
  prior, combine it with the 20 at-bats, and compare your estimate with the raw
  .400. Then check real players: does your Bayesian estimate from early-season data
  predict their end-of-season average better than their early average does?
