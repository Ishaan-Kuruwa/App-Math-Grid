title: "Measuring what a policy does by looking right at the cutoff"
method: regression
field: public-policy
status: reviewed
difficulty: intermediate
summary: >-
  Many rules have a cutoff: a benefit for people over a certain age, extra council
  seats for towns above a certain population, or control of a city government for
  the party that wins more than 50% of the vote. People just above and just below a
  cutoff are usually very similar, so comparing them is almost like running a fair
  experiment. A method called "regression discontinuity" fits a regression line on
  each side of the cutoff and measures the jump between them; that jump is the
  policy's effect. Researchers have explained how to use this design with health
  rules and eligibility cutoffs, used close local elections in Sweden to measure
  whether the winning party changes taxes, spending, and unemployment, and shown
  what can go wrong when towns in France, Germany, and Italy face several rules at
  the same population cutoff.
starterQuestion: >-
  Find a rule with a cutoff, such as a scholarship for students above a test score
  or extra funding for towns above a certain population. Using real or simulated
  data, plot the outcome against the variable that decides eligibility, fit a
  separate straight line on each side of the cutoff, and measure the jump. Does the
  jump get bigger or smaller if you only use data close to the cutoff?
prerequisites:
  - "Algebra (equations of lines)"
  - "Scatter plots and lines of best fit"
  - "A spreadsheet or basic coding"
